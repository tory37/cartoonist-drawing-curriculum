Warm-ups 6
warmuplines 5 min

Parallel lines
  • Draw a series of straight, parallel lines across the page.
  • Start short — about 3 inches — and let each one grow a little longer than the last, working up to the full width of the page.
  • Keep the spacing and angle consistent as the lines lengthen.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

warmuplines 5 min

Ghosting
  • Mark two points anywhere on the page.
  • Before committing, “ghost” the stroke — replay the arm motion just above the paper a few times to rehearse the line.
  • Draw it in one confident pass, aiming to hit both the start and end point precisely.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

warmuplines 5 min

Lines through a point
  • Mark a single point on the page.
  • Ghost each stroke the same way as in the Ghosting drill.
  • Aim to pass straight through the point somewhere in the middle of the line, not at its start or end.
  • Repeat from many angles until the point is surrounded by lines radiating through it.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

warmupcurves 5 min

Curves through multiple points
  • Place several points on the page, spaced out to follow the curve you intend to draw.
  • Draw a smooth, accelerating curve through them, treating each point as a waypoint to pass through rather than a place to stop.
  • It's fine to draw the curve in segments — rotate the page and let your wrist and fingers move naturally for each one.
  • AvoidStopping at each point, which creates edges and corners instead of a smooth curve. Also avoid drawing slowly or hesitantly — that produces fuzzy, hairy lines. Commit to each segment so the curve can be repeated at high quality.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

warmupellipses 5 min

Ellipse & minor axis
  • Alternative orderDraw the minor axis first, then place the ellipse over it — rotate the page to find the best angle for your hand. Either way, check that the ellipse is symmetrical and that the axis stays centered and perpendicular to it; a lopsided ellipse and an off-axis line are the two most common mistakes.
  • Draw a freehand ellipse, moving your whole arm rather than just your wrist.
  • Keep the line light so it can be cleaned up later with an ellipse guide.
  • AvoidDarkening the ellipse by retracing it, even to fix a bad one — repeating the stroke only makes the flaw more obvious. Redraw it fresh instead.
  • Check that the ellipse has no flat spots and isn't lopsided.
  • Place the minor axis on the ellipse — the line across its narrow dimension that splits it into two equal halves. Getting this right matters for placing the ellipse in perspective.
  • Double-check with an ellipse guide, or fold the paper along the minor axis and hold it up to the light to confirm the two halves line up.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

warmupellipses 5 min

Ellipses between two lines
  • Draw a minor axis line, then a line to its left and one to its right.
  • AvoidDrawing the two outer lines asymmetrically about the minor axis — if they aren't symmetrical, no ellipse can actually fit between them.
  • Place a series of ellipses on the minor axis, matching each one's width to the two outer lines at that point.
  • Vary the degree of the ellipses — how narrow or wide they are — as you go, not just their size.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

Perspective boxes 2
perspective 15 min

1-point perspective boxes
  • Draw a horizon line and choose a vanishing point on it.
  • Draw a rectangle, then connect each of its corners to the vanishing point.
  • Draw a second, smaller rectangle between those connecting lines, farther away — that’s your box.
  • Build the whole box with light construction lines first.
  • Darken the box's inside edges, then darken its outline darkest of all.
  • Retrace each line several times to build up different line weights.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

perspective 15 min

2-point perspective boxes
  • Draw the horizon line, then draw a vertical line for the box's front corner — this establishes the X, Y, and Z axes.
  • Extend the X-axis and Y-axis lines from the bottom of that vertical until they hit the horizon line. Where they cross the horizon gives you the left and right vanishing points.
  • Alternative to steps 1–2Place the left and right vanishing points on the horizon first, then draw the vertical and connect them afterward — instead of the corner's angle defining where the vanishing points fall.
  • Draw lines from the top of the vertical to both vanishing points. Add two more verticals at any distance — one to the left, one to the right.
  • From the tops of those two new verticals, draw lines to the opposite vanishing points. Where they cross gives you the box's back-top corner.
  • Do the same from the bottoms of those two new verticals to the opposite vanishing points. Where they cross gives you the back-bottom corner — connect it to the back-top corner to draw the hidden vertical edge.
  • Darken the box's visible edges. The lighter construction lines should still show through.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

Dividing & multiplying 5
construction 10 min

Dividing a rectangle in half, in perspective
  • Draw the rectangle, staying inside your cone of vision so the perspective doesn't distort unexpectedly.
  • Draw both diagonals, connecting opposite corners. Keep them light — they should disappear in the final drawing.
  • Draw a line through the point where the diagonals cross to divide the rectangle in half, following the perspective grid.
  • NoteThe two halves are still equal, but in perspective the nearer one will look wider than the farther one — that's foreshortening, not a mistake.
  • Repeat the technique on each half to find finer subdivisions, like quarters or sixteenths.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

construction 10 min

Duplicating a rectangle, in perspective
  • Draw the rectangle and decide which direction to multiply toward. Since the height stays the same, extend the two lines that run in that direction.
  • Find the midpoint of that extended line — using diagonals, or by eye if the line is horizontal or vertical.
  • Draw a diagonal from the rectangle's far corner through that midpoint, and continue it until it crosses the extended line.
  • TipOf the two possible diagonals in step 3, pick the shorter one to draw — a shorter hand-drawn line is more precise.
  • Draw a line parallel to the rectangle's edge from that intersection point — that's the far boundary of the duplicated rectangle.
  • TipThis technique multiplies in any direction, not just the one shown here.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

construction 15 min

Multiplying & dividing rectangles
  • Draw a lower and an upper line converging toward a common vanishing point.
  • Connect them with two parallel lines to create a rectangle.
  • Multiply that rectangle toward or away from you using the duplication technique — each new rectangle foreshortens automatically.
  • TipRotate the page to find the best arm position for each straight line. With practice, a tick mark is enough — you won't need to draw the full construction line every time.
  • AvoidAdding multiple lines while correcting a mistake, trying to find the right spot. That only darkens the drawing and draws attention to the uncertainty. Draw one line, make an educated guess, and correct from there.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

construction 15 min

Multiplying & dividing boxes
  • Build a base box, then stack more boxes on top of it or beside it, using the same multiplying/dividing technique you'd use on a flat rectangle.
  • Draw through each box — showing its hidden edges — wherever it helps you double-check the construction.
  • If two lines don't meet where you expect, trace back through the construction to find exactly where it started to misalign, rather than guessing.
  • NoteBeing deliberate about tracking down a misalignment, rather than skipping past it, is what actually speeds up learning this.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

construction 10 min

Dividing into odd-numbered proportions
  • Define the plane you want to subdivide.
  • Draw a line parallel to the horizon, starting at the plane's front edge, and divide it into however many equal segments you need (5, for example).
  • Connect the last subdivision point to the far end of the plane's front edge, and extend that line until it reaches the horizon — where it lands is a new vanishing point. Every line parallel to it converges there too.
  • From each remaining segment point, draw a line to that new vanishing point.
  • Wherever those lines cross the plane's actual front edge, draw a vertical line upward — that transfers the subdivisions onto the plane itself.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

Mirroring in perspective 5
mirroring 10 min

Mirroring horizontal planes
  • Draw the rectangle you want to mirror, plus a perpendicular mirror-plane line. Extend the rectangle's width lines until they reach the mirror plane.
  • Draw the rectangle's diagonals to find its midpoint, then draw a line from that midpoint, in perspective, to the mirror plane.
  • Use that mirror point to mirror the rectangle's near line across the mirror plane with the duplication technique, then do the same for the far line.
  • Connect the two mirrored lines — you now have a mirrored plane. The same technique works for any other parallel-plane construction.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

mirroring 10 min

Mirroring vertical planes
  • Draw the vertical rectangle you want to mirror, then draw its diagonals to find the midpoint of the mirror plane.
  • Extend the rectangle's width dimensions toward the expected position of the mirrored rectangle, and find that rectangle's centerpoint too.
  • Complete the construction with diagonals to find the height of the mirrored rectangle, then darken its final lines.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

mirroring 10 min

Mirroring offset planes
  • Set up a plane that hovers above the ground or mirror plane, then extend the lines at each of its corners toward the mirror direction.
  • Mirror the plane's front line across the mirror plane using the multiplication technique.
  • Complete the mirrored plane by following the perspective grid, using vertical lines to define its size.
  • Darken the outer edges.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

mirroring 15 min

Mirroring tilted planes
  • Set up the tilted plane and the plane you'll mirror it across, using a perspective grid to keep both located clearly in space, relative to each other.
  • Pick a point on the tilted plane to mirror. Extend both the tilted plane's edge and the mirror plane's edge until they intersect, and drop a vertical from the top of the tilted plane down to the ground plane if it isn't already there.
  • Use the multiplication technique to mirror that point across the mirror plane.
  • Connect the intersection point from step 2 to the newly mirrored point — the plane's angle has now been mirrored in perspective.
  • Follow the perspective grid's guidelines to the vanishing point to transfer a few more mirrored points, then connect them to complete the mirrored plane.

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling

mirroring 25 min

Mirroring rotated, tilted planes
  • Look at all four points of the tilted, rotated plane you want to mirror — since it's both tilted and rotated, at most two points share a height, and none share depth or width.
  • Mirror the plane's top-front point across the mirror plane using the rectangle duplication technique.
  • Extend the plane's tilted centerline until it crosses its extended tilted front edge.
  • Connect that crossing point to the top-front point you already mirrored.
  • From the plane's lower-front corner, draw a line perpendicular, in perspective, to the mirror plane — where it crosses the line from the previous step is the mirrored lower-front corner.
  • Find the mirrored ground line by extending the line along the ground from the lower-front corner until it reaches the mirror plane.
  • Clip that ground line to its correct length: extend the line that's perpendicular to the mirror plane and runs to the rectangle's lower-back edge, and use where it crosses the mirrored line as the endpoint.
  • Repeat the same technique to find the direction and length of the upper edge.
  • Find the upper edge's endpoint by extending the upper line at the back of the construction.
  • Connect the remaining open edges — you've now mirrored a plane that was both tilted and rotated.
  • Darken the edges of both planes.
  • NoteThree points define a plane, so the fourth has to be worked out to actually sit on it — it's easy to forget that and end up with a shape that couldn't physically exist. (M.C. Escher did this on purpose.)

From How to Draw, by Scott Robertson & Thomas Bertling
