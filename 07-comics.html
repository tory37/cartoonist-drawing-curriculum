Draw Your Own Comics

07Comics: paneling & storytelling

Brunetti’s Cartooning: Philosophy and Practice, Scott McCloud, Etherington Brothers · ongoing

This is where everything else pays off — and where a course stitched from free links alone falls apart. It can teach panel grammar, but not how to actually finish something. This module borrows its structure from Ivan Brunetti’s Cartooning: Philosophy and Practice (the book from module 01): a real, classroom-tested escalation from a doodle to a finished short comic, instead of jumping straight from theory to “draw a minicomic.”

Learn the grammar

  • Scott McCloud, Making Comics & Understanding Comics — the standard texts on panel transitions, gutters, pacing, and expressive acting (library, not free).
  • Etherington Brothers’ free “How to THINK when you draw JUNIOR — How to draw COMICS” — a free 50-page ebook walking through a full comic project in short daily sessions.

Script it before you draw it

A comic is written before it’s drawn. Learn the basics of a comic script: panel-by-panel descriptions, keeping dialogue to roughly 25 words a balloon, and controlling pace with panel count — many small panels reads as fast and urgent, a few big ones slows a moment down. Then write one full page yourself before pencilling anything.

Thumbnail before you commit

Working cartoonists redraw a page’s layout several small, rough ways before picking one — it’s a real, teachable skill, not something that just happens. Frank Santoro’s free Layout Workbook column breaks down how professional page compositions actually work.

Stage each panel, not just the page

Santoro’s workbook is about the whole page; staging is about each individual panel — where you put the camera, what’s in the foreground, how a silhouette reads at a glance. Marcos Mateu-Mestre’s Framed Ink (~$25, the book from module 01) is the standard reference working storyboard and comic artists use for exactly this — shot choice, staging, and visual clarity, one panel at a time.

Letter it

Lettering and balloon placement are their own skill, not an afterthought — a badly placed balloon breaks a reader’s flow no matter how good the art is. The Center for Cartoon Studies’ free Expressive Lettering and Balloons handout is a real classroom exercise, not something we invented.

Build the ladder

Instead of one big, scary “draw a minicomic” leap, finish a series of small things, each one all the way through — pencils, inks, letters — before moving up. This is Brunetti’s own structure:

“If you can draw a smiley face and a stick figure, you can start drawing comics.”
the ladder One single-panel gag → one 4-panel strip → one full page → a 4-page minicomic → an 8-page minicomic. Finish each rung before climbing — the finishing habit matters more than the page count.

Study the real thing

All three shows had real comic-book runs published by BOOM! Studios’ all-ages KaBOOM! imprint, drawn by identifiable people whose process is documented:

  • Adventure Time — Shelli Paroline & Braden Lamb, the Eisner-winning art team, describe splitting script → layout → pencils → inks → color between two people.
  • Bravest Warriors — Ian McGinty drew the series for about a year alongside writer Kate Leth.
  • Regular Show — Allison Strejlau was the series artist, alongside writer KC Green.

Read a few actual issues and copy a page panel-for-panel to see how someone working in exactly this style solves layout and acting problems.

Get feedback

Practice without feedback plateaus. Post one finished piece somewhere real people will critique it — r/ArtCrit or a comics-specific Discord — instead of only judging your own work. Be honest with yourself that this is the weakest link in a free curriculum: a genuinely consistent feedback loop is mostly gated behind a paid critique tier — a pattern across almost every free-to-start art platform. Free communities are worth using anyway — they’re just less reliable than that.
